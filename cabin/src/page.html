A mountain getaway, built for family and friends, but most importantly, built for snow. Two bedrooms, one shower, and a sunny living room that takes up most of the floor, with plenty of space for long dinners and slow mornings. Kept warm by propane, a crackling wood stove, or both.

                  Good to know

                  Snow

                  Mountain counties set their own snow load, often 40 to 120 pounds per square foot or more depending on elevation and town. The model lets you pick 60, 100 or 150. Rafter spacing, headers and pier footings change with it. An engineer sizes the real members.

                  Heat

                  My rough estimate is 15,000 to 19,000 BTU an hour at 0 to −15°F. The 21,500 BTU propane furnace turns down to 8,200, so it fits. A wood stove of 25,000 to 35,000 BTU will run hot; burn small, hot fires. A three-week winter stay uses roughly 60 to 90 gallons of propane (my estimate).

                  Walls and roof

                  Fiber-cement board and batten, not metal, which holds up in wildfire zones. The roof is a true cold roof: an air channel above the lower deck keeps the metal cold so snow does not melt and refreeze at the eave. Nothing sits under the low north eave except snow.

                  Between trips

                  Every water line runs inside the insulated floor, never in an outside wall. Drain the house at the low-point valve under the kitchen sink, blow out the lines, and add RV antifreeze to the traps before you leave it cold.
